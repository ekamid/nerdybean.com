title: "The Old Ways: A Journey on Foot"
author: Robert Macfarlane
year: "2012"
genre: nature writing
thought: Walking is a way of reading a landscape, and a way of being read by it.
reflection: >-
  Macfarlane follows ancient paths across Britain and beyond: chalk tracks,
  sea roads, holloways worn deep by centuries of feet, and a mountain
  pilgrimage on the Tibetan plateau. It is part travel writing, part history, part meditation on why
  walking helps people think. I read it after a winter of short, wet runs and
  it made me want to go further, slower.
stayed: >-
  The sense that a path is a kind of collaboration across time between
  everyone who has ever used it.
margin: >-
  I now notice desire paths everywhere: the shortcuts across grass that people
  make simply by choosing them, again and again.
quotes:
  - text: Paths are the habits of a landscape.
    note: >-
      Five words that changed how I see every trail, and also how I think
      about habits in general. Repetition literally shapes the ground.
sections:
  - heading: Why a book about walking landed so hard
    paragraphs:
      - >-
        Living in Wales, I am never far from old routes over hills and along
        coasts. This book gave those walks a depth I had not noticed. The path
        is not just a way from A to B. It is evidence of countless earlier
        decisions about where people wanted to go.
      - >-
        The chapters in high mountains matched something I felt in the Himalayas:
        that at a certain point the walking becomes the thinking, and the
        landscape starts doing some of the work.
  - heading: What it left behind
    paragraphs:
      - >-
        A slower pace on hills, fewer headphones, and more attention to what
        is underfoot. Also an unexpected parallel with software: the
        interfaces people actually use are desire paths, and good design
        notices where the grass is worn.
